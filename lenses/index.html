HOW GLASSES FIX BLURRY VISION

Your eye focuses light onto the retina. When it focuses in the wrong place, the world blurs, big things less than small ones, just like the chart above. A lens in front of the eye moves the focus back. Below is an eye you can break and fix.

What a lens does

A lens bends light by different amounts across its face. One that is thicker in the middle (a plus lens) bends parallel light toward a single point: its focal point. One that is thicker at the edges (a minus lens) spreads light out, as if it came from a point in front of the lens.

Lens strength is measured in diopters: one over the focal length in metres. A +2 D lens focuses at ½ m, or 50 cm; a +4 D lens at 25 cm. The useful thing about diopters is that they add up. Stack a +2 and a +1 and you get +3. That is why a prescription can be a single number: it is how much power to add to, or take away from, your eye.

How a sharp eye focuses

The cornea, the clear front of the eye, does about two thirds of the focusing, and the lens inside does the rest. Together they make about +60 D, which brings distant light to a point 22 mm back: right where the retina is in a typical eye.

Light from something close is still spreading out when it arrives, so it would focus further back. To make up for it, a ring of muscle lets the lens inside the eye bulge and get stronger. This is accommodation. Reading a phone at 40 cm takes 2.5 D of it, since 1 ÷ 0.4 m = 2.5.

When the eye switches to the phone, the lens in the diagram fattens and the street goes soft. Your own eyes do this all day. You just never notice the background blur, because you're not looking at it.

Nearsighted: the eye is too long

In a nearsighted (myopic) eye, the eyeball is slightly too long for its focusing power. Distant light comes to a point in front of the retina and has spread out again by the time it lands.

Close things are fine. Their light arrives spreading out, which pushes the focus back onto the retina. An eye at −2.50 D sees sharply out to 1 ÷ 2.5 = 40 cm, and everything beyond that blurs. Focusing harder can't help: accommodation only ever adds power, and this eye already has too much.

The fix is a minus lens that spreads the light out a little before it enters the eye. A −2.50 D lens makes distant light arrive as if it came from 40 cm away, which this eye can handle.

With glasses that are too strong, a young eye still sees sharply, but only by focusing all the time to cancel the extra minus. Watch the focusing effort bar. That constant work is one cause of eye strain and headaches.

Farsighted: the eye is too short

A farsighted (hyperopic) eye is too short, so distant light would focus behind the retina. A young eye hides this by accommodating constantly, even for distant things, which is why many farsighted children pass a basic eye chart test.

The trouble is that the same focusing budget then has to cover close work too. At +2.50 D and age 20 there is plenty to spare. At 45 the eye can just about manage the distance, with nothing left over for a phone.

A plus lens supplies the missing power up front, so the eye's own focusing is free for close work again.

Astigmatism: two focal points

An astigmatic cornea is shaped less like a ball and more like the back of a spoon: curved more steeply in one direction than the other. Light spreading in one direction focuses at a different depth from light spreading in the other. Instead of one focal point there are two focal lines, and every point in the world smears into a short streak.

On the eye chart, some strokes stay crisp while others blur. In the corner of the diagram, the single point of light stretches into an ellipse or a line.

The fix is a cylindrical lens, curved in one direction and flat in the other, so it adds power along one line only. The axis in a prescription says which way that line points. Get it wrong and the lens barely helps, or even adds an astigmatism of its own.

Presbyopia: when focusing runs out

The lens inside the eye keeps growing and stiffening throughout life, so how much it can change shape shrinks every year. Children have more than 12 D to work with. From the mid-40s it gets hard to muster the 2.5 D needed to read at 40 cm, and by about 50 it's out of reach: people start holding their phone at arm's length.

Reading glasses are simply a plus lens that does the close focusing instead. The catch is that with them on, distance goes blurry, because now there's too much power for far away. Bifocals and progressive lenses fix that with a stronger zone at the bottom of the lens, where your eyes point when you read.

Why squinting helps, and night makes it worse

When focus is off, each point in the world becomes a blur patch on the retina in the shape of the pupil. Light goes through every part of the pupil, and each part lands in a slightly different place. A smaller opening makes a smaller patch. That's why looking through a pinhole sharpens almost any blurry vision, and why squinting, which turns the opening into a slit, helps too.

It works the other way at night. Pupils open to 7 or 8 mm in the dark, the patches grow, and a mild prescription that's easy to ignore by day suddenly turns street signs to mush.

A squint only narrows the opening top to bottom, so it mostly fixes vertical blur. The point of light in the diagram flattens into a sideways line.

Reading your prescription

A glasses prescription has a row for each eye: OD for the right, OS for the left. Each row has up to four numbers. Type in one of yours, or any example, to see what each one means.

Prescriptions sometimes use plus cylinder instead (common from ophthalmologists). To convert: add the cylinder to the sphere, flip the cylinder's sign, and turn the axis by 90°.

What this simulation leaves out

  • The eye is a "reduced eye": one focusing surface of 60 D, 22.3 mm long when sharp. Near- and farsightedness are modelled as an eye that's too long or too short, the most common cause.
  • Glasses sit right at the eye. Real ones sit about 12 mm in front, which shifts the power you need slightly above ±4 D, and makes minus lenses shrink the view and plus lenses magnify it.
  • Blur is pure geometric optics: each point spreads into the shape of the pupil, plus a rough allowance for diffraction. Real eyes have other small aberrations and some depth of focus.
  • Focusing range follows the low end of normal for each age (Hofstetter's formula); individuals vary by several diopters. The sharpness estimate is a rule of thumb, about 20/50 for 1 D of blur through a 3 mm pupil.
  • Your brain isn't simulated. People with uncorrected vision adapt, and often see better than a blur filter suggests. And you're seeing all this through your own eyes, so if you wear glasses, keep them on.
